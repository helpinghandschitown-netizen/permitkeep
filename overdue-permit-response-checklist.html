Found an overdue permit? Separate the facts from the next actions.

An expired date in a ledger is a signal to investigate, not enough information to decide whether a business may operate. Use this checklist to prepare a clear agency inquiry and keep the follow-up owned.

No assumed grace period.

Do not infer that a pending application, fee payment, or unanswered message extends authorization. Verify the applicable status and operating restrictions with the issuing authority and qualified advisers. This guide does not determine whether you may continue operating.

1. Check the record before changing the date

  • Match the permit type, legal entity, location, and issuing authority against the original record.
  • Check whether the ledger date is an expiration date, filing deadline, inspection date, or internal follow-up target. Keep those meanings distinct.
  • Look for a later issued renewal or official status notice. A colleague's recollection or a payment receipt alone does not establish approval.
  • Leave uncertain facts marked unverified. Do not move the expiration date simply to clear an overdue warning.

2. Prepare an official inquiry

Use contact details obtained independently from the issuing authority's official site or verified documents, not an unsolicited renewal message. Send sensitive identifiers or filings only through the authority's approved channel.

We are checking the current status of our [permit type] for [location]. Our records show [date and what that date represents]. Please confirm the current status, any operating restrictions, the required next steps, and the official process for renewal or reinstatement. If an application is pending, what evidence confirms receipt, completeness, and any authorization during review?

This is a question template, not a claim that renewal or reinstatement is available. The authority may require a different process. Ask for a written reference or official instructions when available.

Read each response for what it actually proves

Keep the exact status wording and its source rather than translating every response into “renewed.” These are organizational distinctions, not legal interpretations:

Submission or payment receipt
Record the receipt date and a secure reference. Ask separately whether the application is complete, whether anything is outstanding, and what establishes current operating authorization.
Pending review or inspection
Keep the item unresolved. Assign the requested action and record any agency-stated deadline separately from your internal follow-up date. Do not invent an approval date.
Issued renewal or official approval
Match the entity, location, permit type, effective dates, and conditions to the record. Resolve discrepancies before changing the working deadline.
Conflicting, incomplete, or no response
Retain both source references and mark the status unverified. Request clarification through a verified official channel and escalate the uncertainty; silence is not permission.

3. Give the response a named owner

  • Assign one person to contact the authority and a backup person for absence.
  • Record when and through which official channel the inquiry was made.
  • Set an internal follow-up date appropriate to the urgency; do not label it an agency deadline.
  • Escalate unresolved operating-status questions to the responsible decision-maker and qualified adviser. A task tracker cannot resolve them.

4. Close the loop with evidence, not a checkbox

Keep submission, payment, inspection, and approval as separate facts. Update the working expiration date only when authoritative evidence supports it. Record the effective date and any conditions separately in your approved records. Preserve the earlier record and the reason for the change under your retention policy.

After correcting the working ledger, export a fresh backup and review other locations for the same missing owner or misunderstood date. Avoid replacing every location's deadline with one assumed schedule.
